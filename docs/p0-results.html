The first run merged in ClickHouse: per chunk, a GROUP BY over (repo, rkey) with argMax by time_us and an ORDER BY, so the server held every key of a chunk and sorted it. That cost 11,921 CPU-seconds, ClickHouse pinned for the whole export. The second run ships the ops as they are: crawl rows and firehose creates and updates with their subject, and firehose deletes without touching their JSON. The loader sorts each chunk's ops by (actor, rkey, time_us) and keeps each key's latest, with a delete winning a tie. That sort is real work now (172 thread-seconds against 6, since the first run's chunks arrived sorted), and holding a chunk's ops whole takes the out pass's peak RSS from 4.2 to 10.4 GB. Ordering matters: 1,841 keys were deleted and then created again under the same rkey, so applying all creates and then all deletes would lose them.

Mapping DIDs to ids changed too. The first run looked up every subject at random in the 645 MB directory, about 350 ns each. The second partitions a chunk's subjects by their top 12 bits in one linear pass and looks them up bucket by bucket, so each bucket's ~160 KB slice of the directory stays in cache. Repos keep a run cache, since each table scans in (repo, rkey) order. DID mapping went from 1,056 to 506 thread-seconds. A full sort with a galloping merge-join, tried first, spent about as long sorting as the random lookups it replaced.

RowBinary over Native: Native was 8% smaller on the merged chunk and no faster on the server, and RowBinary streams row by row with a short reader. The loader reads files rather than ClickHouse directly. Ids are positions in the sorted directory, so the directory has to see every DID before the first out log can be written, and streaming would mean reading ClickHouse twice. The build is 1.9 of 16 minutes, so overlapping its directory pass with the export would save at most 23 s.

The parts the design priced came in close: rkeys at 4.94 B against 4.9, and in sets under the estimate at 2.09 B per pair, because partitioned Elias-Fano picks each block's width from its own density. The miss is the by-subject index. The design guessed that lists over a few thousand records were under 0.1% of actors, and they're close: 65,501 of 38,862,638 lists, 0.17%. But those lists hold 31% of all follow records, so a 4 B position per record in them costs 3.67 GB. What it buys is in the Reads section.

The longest out log has 886,861 follows. The longest in set has 31,205,049 followers, 16x the next one (1,908,966). The top one is the account every new account follows by default, so its in set is the network's signup history; it's still one list, decoded block by block like any other.

Engine lookups on ids meet the point-read budget: a miss is one in-set probe, and a hit adds the scan or by-subject lookup that finds the record's rkey. The API's extra cost is the DIDs. Each one is parsed, packed and looked up in the directory (350 ns: the radix table, then a few cache lines of the sorted array), and the answer is formatted back into a URI. In relationships that's 51 lookups, which is most of the 33.9 µs. A P1 API that keeps ids across calls, or a cache of hot DIDs, would close it.

Metrics cost +102 ns on a 1725 ns has on one core, and 9% of all-thread throughput (26.5M/s against 29.2M/s compiled out). That's after sharding the histograms per thread. The first run used the prometheus crate's own histograms, where every thread adds to the same atomics: all-thread has was 12M/s with them against 18M/s compiled out, a third lost to one cache line moving between cores. Tracing off is one relaxed load per op and doesn't show. 1% doesn't show on one core (+19 ns) and costs 2% at 26M ops/s on 32 threads, where 1% is still 265k spans a second into the exporter's queue. 100% adds 1165 ns per op and drops all-thread throughput to 8.2M/s.

The default is 1% when an OTLP endpoint is configured, off otherwise. P1 traces requests, not engine ops: a request that hydrates a feed makes one relationships call, so 1% of requests is a few hundred spans a second at appview scale, which costs nothing measurable here.

96.62% of the deletes since the crawl find their record by URI and return its subject. The 3,182,467 that don't have no create anywhere in the archive, crawl or firehose. They're records created before the firehose table starts (2025-12-19) that the crawl doesn't hold, in two ways:

  • Deleted during the crawl. The crawl ran for 15 hours from 07:08 UTC on Feb 19, and a repo crawled after one of its deletes is rightly without the record. Week 0 holds 88,323 of the unresolved; one bulk-follow account alone deleted 1,217 follows that day.
  • Repos the crawl lists without their records. Sampled unresolved deletes from March to November belong to repos that crawl_repos has as active but that have no follow records in crawl_records, though their deleted rkeys date from 2024 and 2025. That's the steady 40k-190k a week.

That's the same gap vlDB P1 found. marco keeps a tombstone for each, so a create replayed after its delete stays deleted.

The replay also loads the mutable path: 90,975,609 creates into per-list deltas, then every delete. After it, 3,720,917 of 3,720,917 touched actors are back at their snapshot counts. That's every one of them, which is what replaying a create and then its delete has to do.

Every sort in the build is bounded by a count, not by the graph: a file's records (one repo-prefix chunk) in the out pass, one spill sub-bucket in the in pass, one dedup shard in the directory. So at 10x nothing has to spill that doesn't already; the knobs are more chunks, more sub-buckets and more shards, and the box needs ~1.5 TB of disk for export, spill and snapshot at once. The one phase whose memory grows is the directory, at up to 32 B per distinct DID before a dedup. What doesn't scale on this box is serving: the in sets, indexes and directory alone would be ~78 GB, past its 61 GB.

Where P0 differs from the design

  • Ids are positions in the sorted directory for a bulk build, not arrival order. A bulk load has no meaningful arrival order, and sorted ids let the directory skip a hash index: a 16 MB radix table over the sorted packed DIDs finds one in a couple of cache misses. DIDs that arrive after the snapshot get the next ids in arrival order, and a snapshot written from a graph keeps every id (the directory then carries id columns).
  • did:plc packs by ASCII rank, not base32 value. Packed order is then string order, so one sort serves both, and the repo-prefix export chunks map to contiguous id ranges.
  • The by-subject index costs 1.2 B/edge, not ~0. Long lists are rare but hold 31% of follow records. The index is what keeps has and relationships flat on mass-follow accounts; the threshold is a build flag.
  • Snapshots are 64 shard files plus a directory file. Per the updated design: a change rewrites only its dirty shards (P1). Every file carries the build id and an xxh3 per section.
  • Followers come back in id order with an id cursor; out logs newest-first with an rkey cursor. As decided. Non-TID rkeys sort as newest.
  • Tombstones live in memory only. They're dropped once every source cursor passes them, which needs P1's cursors and WAL. A snapshot doesn't carry them yet.
  • Writes take &mut self, and deltas are BTreeMaps. P0 measures the engine; the lock-free epoch swap per list is P1. Delete by URI runs at 2.4 µs p50 and 8.7 µs p99 against the 5 µs p99 target, mostly URI parsing, DID interning and tree inserts, so P1's delta layout has that to win.
  • The archive's merge moved into the loader. ClickHouse only filters and ships ops; the loader keeps each key's latest by time_us in the sort it does anyway. It halves the export's ClickHouse CPU and makes the deletes for the replay a by-product of the build.
  • The loader reads export files, not ClickHouse directly. See Bootstrap: sorted ids need every DID before the first out log, so streaming would be the export done twice.

Method

  • Box. One 32-thread desktop (16 cores), 61 GB of RAM, one NVMe drive, Linux 7.0. Built for x86-64-v3, release with thin LTO.
  • Source. The archive's merge, per collection and repo-prefix chunk: the February crawl's rows as creates at their time_us, unioned with the firehose table's events, the latest op per (repo, rkey) by time_us, keys whose latest op is a delete dropped. Subjects are JSONExtractString(record_json, 'subject'). This is the network's current state, so it has more follows than the February crawl the design sized against.
  • Build. marco-load build, caches dropped first, 64 shards, by-subject index at 4,096 records. Peak RSS is VmHWM, reset per phase.
  • Reads. marco-load bench on a warm page cache: 10,000 random actors with at least one follow, the 20 most-followed and most-following, half of the has pairs real edges and half random. One warm-up round, then three rounds per tracing setting with the order rotated; each op reports the round with the median mean. Latencies are per op on one thread, timed one at a time; throughput runs on all threads.
  • Overhead. The same bench from a build with the metrics feature off, and with tracing at 0, 1% and 100% exporting OTLP/HTTP to a sink on the ops port.
  • Checks. marco-load verify (checksums, every list decoded, pairs both ways, a separately exported sample and per-repo counts) and marco-load replay.
  • Reproduce. The export and run scripts are in internal/ (not exported); internal/p0_report.py writes this page from the raw JSON.
