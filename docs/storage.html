marco storage engine: no mmap, no page cache

Design and phase plan · written 2026-11-05, revised the same day after an independent review · status: proposed, not built

design record (internal)

marco reads its snapshot through mmap. That turns a bad disk read into a SIGBUS, a page fault into a stalled tokio worker, and leaves the kernel's 4 KiB LRU with readahead as the only cache. This design replaces it with one engine:

  • Snapshot pages are read with O_DIRECT into memory marco owns.
  • A pinned tier holds what every request touches.
  • A scan-resistant page cache sits under --memory.
  • Every request starts on its tokio worker. A request that misses moves whole to a bounded blocking pool, so no runtime thread waits on a disk.
  • Every page read from disk is checked against its checksum.
  • Checkpoints write only the pages that changed, as patch files next to an immutable base. Cached pages stay valid across a checkpoint, and the switch fits inside the budget.

When the budget holds the whole snapshot, everything loads at open and the same code never misses.

1. Open questions

Q1. What --memory covers. The budget caps the snapshot store: the pinned tier (immutable arrays read from the files), page frames, retired frames waiting on the epoch, and the engine's own tables. Everything that grows with writes sits outside it:
  • the deltas, for three graph copies while a checkpoint runs (the two served copies and the checkpoint's own replay copy);
  • live DIDs;
  • the tombstone and stamp maps;
  • the request heap.
At the archive day's rate that comes to about 1.1 GB of deltas (2.48M delta entries at their peak on the public instance, × ~150 B, × 3 copies) plus the maps.
Recommend: store only. Startup warns when the cgroup limit less --memory is under 2 GiB, and the console shows the rest of the RSS next to the budget.
Q2. One bad page. RPCs that touch a page that failed its checksum (or got EIO three times) get data_loss (or unavailable) naming file and page. Everything else keeps serving.
Recommend: /readyz stays ready for isolated bad pages and an alert pages, because the fix (a rebuild or a restored file) needs a human. Readiness drops only on an EIO storm: 64 distinct pages failing within 60 s, which means the disk is going.
Q3. Full residency gets about 10% bigger. FORMAT 6 needs about 43.8 GB to hold the whole network with list items in memory, against the 40.0 GB snapshot today. The extra comes from page packing, page sums and frame tables, and from a by-subject index that carries its subjects, so Has on a long list reads one page instead of ten.
Recommend: accept it. Under the 48 GiB limit, a 44 GiB budget leaves 3.4 GB of headroom for a checkpoint and 4.3 GB outside the budget for deltas and the process.
Q4. Heat-ordered layout. The builder could lay small lists out by each actor's last write (its newest rkey), so that active accounts share pages.
Recommend: build it as an option in phase 6, and adopt it only if the skewed runs at Tight and Half both show at least 20% fewer misses (E8).
Q5. Compaction when everything fits. Old versions of changed lists stay in memory inside pages that still hold live lists. When the budget holds everything, compaction has to clear about a day's changes (R ≈ 1.2 GB) of that garbage every day so the next checkpoint fits its headroom. With garbage spread evenly over 64 shards, that comes close to rewriting every shard daily: about 40 GB a day, one shard at a time, rate-limited. That is the same volume a checkpoint writes today, spread out and never over budget. Below full residency, garbage pages drop out of the cache on their own, and compaction runs only for disk space: a few shards a day.
Decided (decision 67): accept it. At full residency compaction rewrites close to every shard daily, about 40 GB a day of rate-limited writes, and Fits gets no extra headroom.

3. Architecture

The Graph keeps its shape: two copies, deltas in memory, the writer swapping copies. Only Base changes. It reads through a Store whose files live in an engine-wide registry. Files never change after they're written, so a cached page is keyed by (file, page) and stays valid until the file is gone. A checkpoint adds patch files and leaves unchanged pages where they were. The checkpoint's replay copy and the next snapshot share every file, its pinned parts and its cached pages. Nothing is mapped from a file. The frame arena is anonymous memory with MADV_HUGEPAGE, committed and released in 2 MiB groups.

4. FORMAT 6: pages and columns

Three changes do the work:

  • Small lists stay whole. Lists of 128 records or fewer, about 92% of out lists, keep their FORMAT 5 bytes and pack whole into pages. A small list is one page read.
  • Big lists split into columns. Has, BlockPairs, known followers and filters scan subjects and in sets, and never pull in the 13.5 GB of rkeys.
  • Lookup state moves into a pinned head. Everything a lookup needs to find the right block (skips, firsts, offsets, by-subject fences) lives in memory, so every pass can name all the pages it needs before reading any.

The by-subject index changes from positions sorted by subject (4 B a record) to blocks of 128. Each block holds the subjects as Elias-Fano, through the in-set block codec, which takes repeats, plus the positions as u32. FORMAT 5's binary search reads subject(bysubj[mid]) at every step, which on a cold cache is about ten dependent page reads. The new form costs about 5.6 B a record (4.8 GB against 3.4 GB) and makes the lookup one page.

Blocks stay positional (128 records each), as in FORMAT 5. Slack leaves, which would let one edge change rewrite one block, were costed against the real day in §11. They save about 0.6 GB of patch bytes a day, but by-subject would have to key on rkeys instead of positions. That isn't worth it.

Offsets are u32 within a shard's space for each section kind: the base file's pages first, then each patch file's, so a list can move to a patch without its neighbours moving. Lists are self-delimiting and their order is free, which is what the heat-ordered option (Q4) needs. u32::MAX marks an id with no list in a dense index, and the sparse form keeps its bitmap. FORMAT bumps to 6. There is no FORMAT 5 reader and no migration: snapshots are rebuilt from the archive (§15, the cutover).

The page wins because the lookup is the same at every budget: index → offset → slots[page] → frame. That's one dependent load more than an mmap slice, with no hashing and no allocation per object. The column split removes most of the co-location waste. A page of subjects carries only subjects, and big lists, which hold 82% of records, get pages of their own blocks.

S3-FIFO beats W-TinyLFU here on two counts. It's scan-resistant in the way that matters: a page touched once (one big Intersect, a ListIn walk, a cold viewer) dies in the small queue without touching the main one. And it's frequency-aware through 2-bit counters and the ghost, with no sketch and no list update on a hit, only a mutex on the eviction path. That path already costs a disk read.

Which reads go where:

  • Request and writer misses enter the small queue.
  • Patch and compaction pages a checkpoint writes are written through into the small queue.
  • The full-residency load at open fills main directly.
  • Verify, list stats and compaction's own reads of old pages bypass the cache.

Frames, epochs, pins, memory

  • Epochs. A pass reads frames as plain slices under a crossbeam-epoch guard. The evictor clears a frame's slot, then retires the frame. The frame is reused only after every guard that could have seen it is gone. Passes never wait on I/O (§7), so the epoch always moves.
  • Retired-frame reserve. Retired frames count against the budget. The evictor keeps at least max(1,024 frames, 0.5%) free or retired. When the free list is empty, a fetch calls guard.flush(), evicts again, and then waits on a condvar for reclamation. It never allocates past the budget.
  • Pins. A frame's state word holds RESIDENT plus a pin count, changed by CAS. A request that missed pins the pages it touched before dropping its guard. A pin that finds the frame retired or reassigned fails, and that page counts as missing for the next pass. A frame goes back to the free list only when it has no owner and no pins. The last unpin frees a frame whose file was dropped.
  • Pin budget. A request holds at most 2,048 pins (8 MiB), and at most 64 requests run on the miss pool, so pins never exceed 512 MiB. A request that has touched more pages keeps going without new pins, relying on the small queue. If it needs more pages at once than half the small queue holds, it fails with resource_exhausted: "this request needs N MiB of pages at once".
  • Giving memory back. Frames come in 2 MiB groups. To borrow memory for new pinned parts or a compaction, the engine picks the groups with the most free frames, evicts the rest of their frames, and returns each group with MADV_DONTNEED. RSS falls before the new allocation lands, so the "≤ --memory" gates measure RSS and not just the engine's books.

7. The read path

Find the pages, fetch the misses, compute

Structures stay synchronous and slice-based. What changes is where a slice comes from:

// marco-core
pub struct Pending;                                   // a miss; the pass can't finish
pub type R<T> = Result<T, Pending>;
pub struct ReadCx<'g> {
    guard: &'g epoch::Guard,                          // pinned by the caller: no slice outlives it
    engine: &'g Engine,
    missing: RefCell<PageSet>,                        // pages to fetch
    touched: RefCell<PageSet>,                        // kept once anything missed, to pin
    stats: Cell<ReadStats>,
}
impl<'g> ReadCx<'g> { pub fn page(&self, p: PageId) -> R<&'g [u8; 4096]>; }
pub trait Blocks<'g> {
    fn block(&self, cx: &ReadCx<'g>, b: usize) -> R<&'g [u8]>;
    fn need(&self, cx: &ReadCx<'g>, bs: Range<usize>) -> R<()>;   // registers every miss in bs
}
OutList<'g, B: Blocks<'g>> / InList<'g, B: Blocks<'g>>            // head pinned, or the one small-list slice
impl Graph { fn base_out<'g>(&'g self, cx: &ReadCx<'g>, rel, id) -> R<Option<OutList<'g, _>>>; ... }

// one pass; the guard lives on this stack frame and dies with it
pub fn pass<T>(e: &Engine, hold: &mut Hold, f: impl FnOnce(&ReadCx) -> R<T>) -> Result<T, Misses> {
    let guard = epoch::pin();
    let cx = ReadCx::new(e, &guard);
    let r = f(&cx);
    match r { Ok(t) if cx.clean() => Ok(t), _ => Err(cx.pin_touched_and_take(hold)) }  // before guard drops
}
// every blocking reader: miss pool, writer, checkpoint, admin jobs
pub fn read_blocking<T>(e: &Engine, hold: &mut Hold, f: impl Fn(&ReadCx) -> R<T>) -> Result<T, StorageError> {
    loop { match pass(e, hold, &f) { Ok(t) => return Ok(t), Err(m) => e.fetch_blocking(m, hold)? } }
}

Each structure method calls need() first for every block it will read, which it can work out from the pinned head, and only then decodes. A scan misses all its blocks in one pass, not one per round. Batch calls (relationships, block_pairs, filter, the probe loops of intersect) catch Pending per item and carry on with the next, so twenty others missing in twenty lists cost one round, not twenty. In-memory overrides (out_over, in_over) implement Blocks and never miss. A Pending swallowed into a None can't produce a wrong answer, because clean() fails any pass that recorded a miss.

// marco-server handler, e.g. relationships: the whole handler is the closure
let work = move |cx: &ReadCx| -> R<RelationshipsResponse> {
    let g = app.graph();                      // per pass; dropped at the end of it
    build_relationships(&g, cx, &req)          // lookups, hidden(), did_of, the response: all inside
};
match app.engine.pass(&mut Hold::none(), &work) {           // on the tokio worker, never waits
    Ok(resp) => Ok(resp),
    Err(_) => app.miss_pool.run(work).await.map_err(storage_error)?,   // read_blocking on a pool thread
}

The miss pool has 64 threads by default (--miss-threads). Little's law sizes it: a request that misses spends ~0.3-0.6 ms in two or three rounds, so 64 threads carry about 100k missing requests a second. That's above the miss rate the I/O pool can feed (§9).

A handler waits for a pool permit asynchronously, so its worker keeps serving hits. A request that waits longer than 1 s (or its deadline) gets resource_exhausted "storage busy". The queue wait is a metric. A request runs as many rounds as it needs: rounds equal the dependency depth, plus any writes or a checkpoint switch that change what the next pass needs. Nothing turns a slow request into an error. A request whose missing set stops shrinking for 16 rounds is logged and counted, as a bug signal.

9. Async I/O

The default backend is a pool of 64 threads doing blocking preadv on O_DIRECT fds straight into frames. It's fed by two bounded queues: request and writer misses first, then background work. Adjacent pages of one file are coalesced into one read of up to 256 KiB. On this disk it reaches 384k reads/s at ~3.5 µs of CPU a read, which is 0.35 of a core at 100k misses/s, and it builds and runs everywhere.

io_uring (one ring per I/O thread through the io-uring crate, no SQPOLL) would cut the CPU per read to roughly a third, and reach deeper queues from one thread. That pays only above ~150k misses/s. Whether the target load gets there is a measurement, not a guess.

  • misses/s at the sustained rate exceed 150k;
  • I/O CPU exceeds 10% of the server's;
  • queue wait p99 at 128 threads exceeds 200 µs.

10. Writes

An add reads the actor's out list (does the key exist, what did it name) and the subject's in set (is the pair new). A write can't stop halfway through a mutation. So the writer first runs a read-only plan pass over the batch through the same lookups, fetches what's missing, and plans again until a pass comes back clean. Only then does it apply.

The apply goes through read_blocking, and the plan's pins are held until the second copy is done. A miss the plan didn't predict (a status delete's whole out list, say) makes the apply fetch and retry a lookup: read_blocking is idempotent for reads, and the mutation happens after its reads succeed.

The writer does wait on disk: one fetch round per dependency level per batch, on its own OS thread. At Tight a cold batch adds ~0.3-0.6 ms, against the 5 s segment interval. Writes, segments, the two copies and decision 63's cursors don't change.

11. Checkpoints: base plus patches

A checkpoint writes only what changed. For each shard with changes, it writes one patch file. The patch holds the changed small lists whole, the changed suffix blocks of each changed big list's columns (an append changes only the tail block, and an insert or delete changes the blocks from that position on), and that shard's complete new pinned sections: index, heads, and sums for the patch's own pages. The manifest names each shard's base and its patches, and its rename is still the commit. Decision 63 doesn't change: no WAL, cursors in the manifest, replay after it.

Unchanged pages never move, so every cached frame stays valid across the switch with no remap. Pages the checkpoint writes go straight from its buffers into the cache (write-through), so a fully resident server stays fully resident. Old versions of changed lists become garbage inside pages that still hold live lists, until compaction rewrites the shard.

Checkpoints run every 3M ops or 24 h. The public instance ingested 1.20M ops in its last day, so a checkpoint comes daily. Pinned sections add about 1.2 GB a checkpoint (P_new), because every shard's index changes somewhere. A daily checkpoint therefore writes about 2.4 GB, against the 37.7 GB the current snapshot format rewrites. It reads only the old versions of changed lists, through the cache, and takes seconds.

Slack leaves, which let an insert rewrite one block instead of a suffix, would cut the in-set row to 0.24 GB and the delete and by-subject rows to about zero. That saves ~0.6 GB a day, and isn't worth by-subject keying on rkeys.

The switch, step by step

  1. Replay and write. The checkpoint thread's open_replayed gets its files from the registry, so there's no second pinned tier and no second cache. It reads the base versions of changed lists through read_blocking, merges in the deltas, and writes each changed shard's patch with O_DIRECT, written through into the cache.
  2. Commit. The manifest rename, as now.
  3. Open. Every base and every earlier patch comes from the registry. The engine first borrows the memory for each new patch's pinned sections by returning 2 MiB groups (§6), then reads them.
  4. Switch. The writer replays the segments after the cut and publishes, as now. When the last Store holding the old pinned parts drops, they're freed. A Store that a SharedSnapshot (series, verify, list stats) keeps alive across two checkpoints just keeps its files open: frames are keyed by file, so it reads correct pages and nothing needs a third owner.
  5. Compact (the shard figure above). Compaction streams a shard's live lists past the cache, writes a new base with O_DIRECT, writes it through, commits a manifest naming the new base with no patches, and swaps the shard in place. The logical content is identical, so neither the deltas nor the graph copies change. Its extra memory is one shard, at most 0.7 GB.

The switch gate is P_new + R ≤ H, where H is the budget less what's resident. At Fits on the archive day that's 1.2 + 1.2 = 2.4 ≤ 3.4 GB. When the budget doesn't hold everything, the same bytes come out of the cache by eviction, and the patch pages enter the small queue, where only reuse keeps them.

Compaction cost. When everything fits, compaction must clear about R of garbage a day so that G ≤ H − P_new − R = 1.0 GB holds at the next checkpoint. Garbage spreads evenly over shards, so that comes close to compacting every shard daily, about 40 GB of writes, rate-limited between checkpoints (Q5). Below full residency, garbage pages fall out of the cache, and compaction runs only on its disk triggers. Disk use stays under 1.43× live.

Making the baselines fair

  • Evict before each run. Before every mmap run, evict every snapshot file (posix_fadvise DONTNEED, then vmtouch -e). Then check that memory.stat file in the new scope starts near zero, and that io.stat rbytes grows during warm-up. Otherwise pages charged to an earlier scope give mmap free memory.

  • Same conditions for both. The disk numbers in §9 and any E1 run made during the crawl are provisional. The official E1 and E2 for each case run back to back after the crawl, on the same box state.

The skew, and why

AppView reads have two sides, and they're skewed differently. Both weights come from the archive's public records (the latest version of each record), exported by an internal script.

  • Viewers (the actor of relationships, known followers, follows pages, filters): DIDs with at least one record created in the 30 days before the snapshot's cut, weighted by distinct active days (1-30). The read mix is per active user, and accounts that write on more days open the app on more days. Lurkers who never write are missed, and the uniform run covers that cold tail.
  • Others and subjects (feed and thread authors, profiles, block-pair authors, known-followers subjects): weighted by likes received in the last 7 days, plus 1 for any DID that posted. An author shows up in hydrations when their posts are shown, and likes are a roughly steady fraction of views, so likes received tracks exposure. Follower count would over-weight big accounts that have gone quiet. It runs as a sensitivity check at Half only.

Every run reports the share of draws landing on the top 1% and top 10% of weights, and the distinct lists touched per minute, so runs can be compared. If an AppView's traffic ever reaches the public instance, its request logs would be the real thing and would replace these weights.

Write-throughA checkpoint's patches and new bases and a compaction's base are written through a Stage: each paged page is copied into a frame as it's written, a frame nobody can read until Engine::publish makes it the file's page in the small queue. Staged frames and borrowsA stage's frames, and a read past the cache's, can't be given back, so a borrow drains only groups holding none (each group counts its staged frames), and waits up to borrow_timeout for such groups rather than refusing. Staged frames land in most groups, so a stage borrows its file's registration (8 B a page for its cap, plus rounding) when it begins, and register_staged pays from that. A registration that still fails lets the stage go and registers without it; pages a stage let go count in write_through_skipped. A borrow or a group commit may no longer spend the room a borrow in progress will leave over from the groups it drains before they're released: with eight shard writers borrowing at once, that briefly put the store 0.3 MiB over an 8 MiB budget in the memory test. Below full residency, written-through pages sit in the small queue and only reuse keeps them, so a long checkpoint's earlier shards may be evicted before the switch.store::cache, register_through
Older patches' pinned sections (task 1008)register reads a file's footer and its .sums sections only, so a registration costs its sums and slots (8 B a page). The other pinned sections are PinnedSections (Engine::pinned), read once per file and held by the stores that read the file as the directory or as a shard's newest file. Once the switch's old copies drop, the patch before the new one gives its index and heads back. Their xxh3 is checked when they're read; an older patch's are checked only by verify.store::engine::PinnedSections, StoreFile::Paged
The minimum budgetThe pinned tier, plus the shards' newest pinned sections a second time, plus 2 GiB. A checkpoint that patches every shard reads each one's index and heads again beside the old until its switch, but never the directory, and older patches no longer hold theirs, so phase 4's twice the tier comes down by the directory (0.89 GB on the whole network) and whatever older patches held. New pinned sections come out of the cache by the 2 MiB group drains phase 2 built, before they're read. The hidden --max-frames skips the check but no longer the full-residency load.app::App::open_on, Store::shard_pinned_bytes
Compaction's memoryIts new base's pages are written through (frames of the budget), and its reads of the old files go past the cache into frames of its own (below). The builder's column buffers stay outside the budget, as the request heap does.SharedSnapshot::compact
Reads past the cacheHold::past (Engine::read_past): a page already cached is read without counting a use; a missing one is read on the background lane into a frame the hold keeps to itself, never published, freed when the hold drops. A page that fails its sum is read once more before it's marked bad, as a fetch does. If the frames don't come within 50 ms, or the hold would keep more than its job limit (half the cache), the pages are read through the cache instead, so a full cache never stalls the job. Compaction and list stats read this way. Verify already read pages past the cache; its re-read of pinned sections moves to the background lane too.read::Hold::past, Engine::fetch_past, liststats
Background writesA writer paced by the token bucket takes from it once per MiB written, as one operation, and the rest at finish: checkpoint patches and bases, compaction bases. --background-io MB,IOPS (default 200,5000) sets the bucket; it replaces --compaction-rate-mb, and CompactPolicy loses its rate. On mmap the engine has the same bucket for writes.PagedWriter::pace, Engine::background, main.rs
Compaction at full residency (decision 67)When the engine loaded the whole snapshot at open and its paged pages still fit the cache, App::headroom gives the free room (frames neither cached nor retired) and what the next checkpoint will take (the last one's P_new + R). Compaction then also takes shards beyond the garbage policy, most cached garbage first (paged pages no index names, Garbage::pages), until that garbage covers the shortfall; when all of it couldn't, it takes none, since rewriting every shard after every checkpoint wouldn't make the room. Before the first checkpoint the need is 0, so nothing extra runs.Headroom, SharedSnapshot::compaction_due
A bad page under a checkpointThe merge's reads fail the checkpoint naming the page, and the files it wrote are deleted (by name: they're this checkpoint id's alone, and an id the committed manifest uses is refused before anything is written or deleted). Its replay of segments fails it too: an op skipped on a bad page there would be folded into the snapshot and lost. At open, such an op is skipped and logged, and its segment stays until a checkpoint can apply it. At the switch, the new copies are dropped instead and the old ones keep serving (the error is "reopen after commit"), since they applied the op. Segments keep accruing; a verify that reads the page clean lets the next checkpoint through.app::apply_planned, Graph::write_checkpoint
ShutdownThe writer writes the last segment, stops the compaction and waits for a running checkpoint, then replies, so nothing writes the snapshot after shutdown returns. It used to reply first, which let an orphaned checkpoint commit under a reopened snapshot (the durability test's compaction case failed about one run in 150 under load).writer::Writer::wait_checkpoint
Lookups by structureEach space knows its structure from its section name, and a pass counts its lookups by structure and by how they came out (small queue, main, otherwise in memory, missed) in cells, added to the engine's striped counters at the end of the pass.read::Structure, ReadCx::page_of
